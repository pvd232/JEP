## 1. Reparation

Source: [SENIT 8 of 2025](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1tlzKx6Dyy-RMkbUr3-_NOrJkSR2WD1Zm/view). Printed pp. 27-29, plus paragraph 68 and the completion of paragraph 67 on p. 30.

### Reparation as a right and a continuing obligation

The Appeals Section explains reparation through three connected legal roles: a principle of the peace system, a fundamental right of victims, and an obligation of those who caused harm in connection with the armed conflict. The peace agreement combines satisfaction of victims' rights with ending the conflict and preventing its repetition. Access to special criminal treatment depends on contributions to those purposes, and the JEP verifies compliance with the corresponding conditions. The selection develops the meaning of the obligation to contribute to reparation within that arrangement. (Printed pp. 27-29, paragraphs 58-59 and 64.)

### The breadth of the right and the distribution of resources

The judgment connects reparation to victims' dignity and to their rights to truth and justice. It identifies restitution, compensation, rehabilitation, satisfaction, and guarantees of non-repetition as forms through which the right becomes concrete. The needs addressed can be individual or collective and can involve material, moral, and symbolic dimensions. The form of reparation should respond to the characteristics of the people and communities affected. (Printed p. 28, paragraphs 61-62.)

Mass victimization creates practical questions about how resources should be distributed. The judgment discusses the difficulty of applying individual full-compensation standards across a large population of victims and cites the experience of Peru's reparations program. Its reasoning preserves the breadth of the right while explaining why a mass program requires a distribution model that can reach victims fairly. The constitutional provision reproduced in footnote 80 calls for equality of access, equity in allocating available resources, and priority for people entitled to special constitutional protection. (Printed pp. 28-29, paragraphs 63 and 66; footnote 80.)

### The State's duty and appearing parties' contributions

The State must guarantee reparation through public institutions and policies. The peace agreement builds on the existing victims' assistance and reparation system and connects it with programs such as land restitution, territorial development, and collective reparation. Those institutional responsibilities provide the setting in which the contributions of appearing parties must operate. (Printed pp. 29-30, paragraphs 65-67.)

Paragraph 68 describes two practical forms of contribution by actors in the conflict. One consists of collective acknowledgment of responsibility and public requests for forgiveness, accompanied by commitments to concrete reparative action. The other consists of concrete actions whose design and promotion involve the National Government. The constitutional provisions reproduced in footnote 81 retain duties to clarify the truth, contribute to reparation, and guarantee non-repetition for beneficiaries of the specified criminal-law treatments; the provision concerning members of the Public Force expressly describes their reparative contribution as non-monetary. (Printed p. 30, paragraph 68 and footnote 81.)

### Reading implication

A reparative commitment can be examined by identifying the harm it addresses, the victim right or remedy involved, and the actor responsible for carrying it out. The State's duty to guarantee reparation and an appearing party's duty to contribute remain distinct responsibilities within the same institutional arrangement.

## 2. Restoration

Source: [Restorative Transitional Justice Manual (JEP, 2024)](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1g8kIclVgIbMnZW11-CjuPGzWTO4ifwuJ/view). Printed pp. 29-30, 38 and 46.

### Restoration through participation and responsibility

The manual presents restorative justice as a way for people affected by wrongdoing to participate in deciding how its consequences should be addressed. Victims, responsible persons, and communities take part in a process directed toward reparation, acknowledgment of responsibility, and the rebuilding of social relationships. Within the Colombian transition, restorative justice also serves the aims of peace and reconciliation. The selection explains how these purposes enter the JEP's legal framework and its conduct of proceedings. (Printed pp. 29-30 and 38.)

### A process with concrete conditions and outcomes

The manual describes restorative justice both through the process people follow and through the outcomes they seek. As a process, it requires opportunities for participation, deliberation, and communication. The conditions identified include neutral mediation, trust in the authorities, respect for the parties, and a meaningful opportunity to express aspirations, views, and available resources. Responsible persons are expected to confront their conduct and its consequences and acknowledge their responsibility. (Printed p. 30, with the labeled sentence continuation from p. 31.)

The outcomes described include reparation for victims and the restoration of relationships affected by the wrongdoing. Reading a proceeding through this account therefore involves examining how people participate and what their participation helps accomplish. A hearing's procedures, the substance of an acknowledgment, and the proposed response to harm are connected parts of the restorative process. (Printed pp. 29-30.)

### Applying restorative justice to mass victimization

The JEP operates in a setting where violence has affected large numbers of people and communities. The manual identifies collective participation as a practical issue when restorative justice is applied at that scale. It places the JEP's approach within the Final Peace Agreement, constitutional provisions, procedural law, and statutory law. Those sources connect restoration with social relationships, the harm caused, and the rights of future generations. (Printed p. 38, with the labeled sentence continuation from p. 39.)

The 2024 manual reports that its jurisprudential review identified more than 1,000 decisions mentioning restorative justice, selected 35 for their substantive content, and highlighted 10 offering detailed definitions or concepts. These figures describe the manual's review and explain the basis for the account of JEP practice presented in the selection. (Printed p. 38.)

### Dialogue across the course of proceedings

The decisions discussed on page 46 describe dialogue as an ongoing process that develops through successive interactions. The JEP facilitates victims' participation, acknowledgment of truth and responsibility, and the rebuilding of relationships. The account also incorporates gender, differential, and intersectional approaches. The manual links continued participation to the search for truth and reparation and describes consequences for failures to comply with restorative commitments, including expulsion in the cited case of renewed involvement in armed activities. (Printed p. 46.)

### Reading implication

The selection provides a basis for examining who participates, how the institution enables dialogue, what responsibility is acknowledged, and what response to harm follows. Reparation supplies concrete responses to victims' injuries; restoration also concerns the relationships and participatory process through which those responses are pursued.

## 3. Sanctions

Source: [Law 1957 of 2019](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1bhzncJs2S-8Oq5j_pHdkyzt-ZOtg6TDe/view). Articles 125-126, printed p. 34.

### The purposes a sanction must serve

Article 125 gives JEP sanctions two central purposes: satisfying victims' rights and consolidating peace. The sanction's restorative and reparative function concerns the harm caused, and the law connects that function to the degree of acknowledgment of truth and responsibility made before the JEP. The provision therefore places the sanction within a relationship among the offense, its effects on victims, and the responsible person's acknowledgment. (Printed p. 34, Article 125.)

The article identifies three categories of sanction: own sanctions, alternative sanctions, and ordinary sanctions. It also requires the ruling or judgment to state the sanction's content and place of execution precisely, together with the relevant conditions and effects. These requirements make the judicial response concrete: the decision must specify what is imposed and how it will operate. (Article 125.)

### Eligibility, duration, and restrictions for own sanctions

Article 126 develops the category called own sanctions, or *sanciones propias*. It applies to those who acknowledge responsibility and provide exhaustive, detailed, and full truth before the Chamber of Acknowledgment in respect of the very serious offenses described in the provision. The article sets a period of five to eight years for fulfillment of the sanction's reparative and restorative functions. (Article 126, first paragraph.)

The eight-year maximum applies across all own sanctions imposed, including cases involving concurrence of offenses. Own sanctions also entail effective restrictions on freedoms and rights, including residence and movement, as needed for execution. The provision expressly requires a guarantee of non-repetition. Its account of an own sanction thus combines reparative and restorative functions, a defined period, and enforceable restrictions. (Article 126, second paragraph.)

### Reading implication

A description of a proposed own sanction can be examined for the acknowledgment on which it rests, the harm its activities address, its duration, and the restrictions attached to its execution. Article 125 supplies the purposes and required specificity of the judicial decision; Article 126 supplies the conditions and principal features of own sanctions.

## 4. Harm

Source: [Order TP-SeRVR-RC-AI-No.001-2026, Catatumbo](https://drive.google.com/file/d/18k4JNF2D_Z6gqmKTBIDgsFrJU3l7fQSz/view). Printed pp. 792-793, including paragraphs 1947-1949.

### Harm as the connection between victim experience and the judicial response

The Catatumbo decision explains why the JEP must characterize harm when assessing acknowledgment and proposed sanctions. The characteristics of victims and the contexts of the violence help explain differentiated impacts. That understanding then informs the assessment of a proposal's capacity to contribute to restoration and reparation. The selection links the study of harm to acknowledgment and the affirmation of victims' dignity. (Printed p. 792, paragraphs 1945-1946.)

Paragraphs 1947-1949 organize the role of harm around three purposes.

### 1. Assessing the crimes and the patterns of victimization

Characterizing harm helps establish the seriousness, systematic character, and differentiated impact of the crimes. The decision connects this assessment to prioritization, determination of the facts, and selection of those most responsible, from whom acknowledgment will be required. It also treats widespread impacts and shared victim characteristics as a basis for understanding macro-victimization: harm considered across connected experiences and different dimensions, with attention to its systemic nature. (Printed pp. 792-793, paragraph 1947.)

### 2. Giving acknowledgment a restorative dimension

Acknowledgment requires publicly naming the harm caused. The decision explains that acknowledgment can itself contribute to reparation through satisfaction: victims may encounter an official account that recognizes what the conflict took from them, interrupted, or changed for the worse. Such recognition can honor victims and affirm their experience in a judicial setting. (Printed p. 793, paragraph 1948.)

The same paragraph connects acknowledgment to moral accountability. Appearing parties can be confronted with the pain they caused, and the restorative setting can create possibilities for encounters that contribute to peacebuilding. The relevant acknowledgment concerns the consequences experienced by victims as well as responsibility for the acts. (Paragraph 1948.)

### 3. Assessing proposals for restoration and reparation

Understanding the nature of harm provides a guide for assessing sanction proposals and subsequently imposing sanctions that contribute to restoration and reparation. The decision emphasizes systemic harm because appearing parties bear an obligation to contribute to repairing it. Characterization of harm therefore gives the proposed response a substantive point of reference. (Printed p. 793, paragraph 1949.)

### Reading implication

The three purposes support three linked questions when reading testimony: what harm was experienced and how widely it was shared; how that harm was acknowledged; and how a proposed measure responds to it. These questions are an analytical reading aid drawn from the passage. The source develops the legal reasons for connecting victim experience, acknowledgment, and the assessment of sanctions.
